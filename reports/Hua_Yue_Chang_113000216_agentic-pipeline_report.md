# Your report

   In this experiment I ran tools to analyse a dataset of 2257 documents and 35788 features (build_dtm_7), which can be categorised into four categories: soc.religion.christian, sci.med, comp.graphics and alt.atheism (load_dataset_1). Among the 20 tools, 18 are self-made; therefore, access permission is explicitly required when calling the tools. The process can be roughly summarised into 5 parts: loading the data and seeing attributes of the dataset, constructing a document-term matrix and in turn drawing graphs based on it, applying different filters to mine pattern and dimensionality reducing and calculating cosine similarity. The data shows a sparsity of 99.547% (build_dtm_7), meaning that the terms in documents occupy little of the whole vocabulary. 

   Looking closer at the data, there are quite a few noteworthy findings. First, there are no empty documents, single-word documents, missing text and duplicates (inspect_data_2)(check_missing_3)(check_duplicates_4). However, the text length of data varies much, with a maximum length of 60713 and a minimum of 125 (describe_data_5) and 96 length outliers detected with a 3×IQR rule (inspect_data_2). In the box plot by category using describe_data_tool (describe_data_5), soc.religion.christian has the largest mean but all documents have lengths below 30000, whereas comp.graphics having the lowest mean but the longest text among four categories. In addition, analysis with term_frequency_tool (term_frequency_8) shows 19 stop words within the top 20 frequent terms, the exception being a common email component 'edu'.  

   Applying different techniques to data analysis, a few notable results show. After applying the variance filter (variance_filter_10) with a threshold of 0.01, result shows that 18 of 20 retained terms are common words in english, such as 'the', 'and' or 'of', only 'jpeg' is content-related retained terms. While variance filter shows mostly common english words, the comparison between Pearson filter and Spearman filter is rather distinct. In a juxtaposition of the two filters with the same category 'alt.atheism' (pearson_filter_11, spearman_filter_15), we can see the result of pearson filter contains more field-unrelated words such as 'writes', while Spearman contains relatively less, yet problem still remains. Also, Pearson method contains more names of author such as 'keith', 'schneider' or 'allan', whereas Spearman contains less of them and more terms on the topic itself like 'atheists', 'morality' or 'islamic'. Another example compares results of two different filters with the category 'comp.graphics'(pearson_filter_12, spearman_filter_14), revealing the same case where pearson contains more common words like 'thanks', 'looking' or 'hi' while Spearman contains only 'thanks' as common english words. Another thing worth mentioning is that Spearman only calculates the top 1500 variance terms, so the comparison isn't essentially fair. Regarding mine_patterns_tool using tfidf with minSup approximately equals 17% of the category (patterns_17, patterns_18, patterns_19, patterns_20), the result shows mostly noise, with email titles such as 'lines', 'subject' and 'organization' dominating top patterns. However, notably, in the category 'soc.religion.christian', category-specific word 'god' appears in index 15. 

   Lastly, in reduce_dimensions_tool we tried three different methods: PCA, UMAP and t-SNE (reduce_pca_21, reduce_umap_22, reduce_tsne_23). The result is quite distinctive. PCA is heavily influenced by outliers hence hard to differentiate between four categories. With UMAP, it is able to see small clusters of certain categories like 'sci.med' or 'soc.religion.christian' and 'alt.atheism' clustering on the right side, yet the overall clustering remains vague with 'comp.graphics' and 'sci.med' dominating the bottom-right. In t-SNE, 'soc.religion.christian' and 'alt.atheism' intertwine with each other on the top-left likely for sharing the same topic about god and religion, whereas 'comp.graphics' and 'sci.med' thin out more gradually on the right. In addition, after applying cosine similarity on 'comp.graphics', 'soc.religion.christian' and two different categories in cosine_sim_25, cosine_sim_26 and cosine_sim_27, we found that the similarity doesn't differ much whether it's 'same category' or 'different category', from 0.353, 0.302 to 0.340.

   In conclusion, the result shows that header and noises dominate the dataset, such as email headers or stop words. We should try remove those noises before applying these mining techniques.
